title: "Story 01: The Glass Orchard"
date: 2026-06-07

Adrian Roundhouse stood with the brass skeleton key between his fingers and considered whether to let the woman die.

She was curled beneath the east transept fig, where the old panes had gone green at the edges and the floor tiles sweated under a roof that had not kept weather out since before the trial. Her coat had been expensive once. Now it clung to her in dark strips, its hem slick with mud and glass grit. One shoe remained on her foot. The other lay three yards away, heel broken, toe pointed at him as if making an accusation.

Behind Adrian, Julianna waited by the citrus walk.

She wore the white walking dress he had given her in April, though the month outside had become November. She had never complained about this. Julianna did not complain unless he gave her a civic purpose for it, and this morning he had opened the district for quiet visitation only. No petitions. No disputes about fountain maintenance. No schoolchildren climbing the ironwork. A low day. A pure day.

The woman under the fig made a wet sound in her throat.

Adrian tightened his fingers around the key until its teeth printed themselves into his palm.

The east gate was already open. He had opened it at seven precisely, as he did every morning, though the lock had rusted away three winters earlier and the gate itself now sagged on one hinge. Still, the key had turned. It had turned because he had asked the world to behave, and the world, inside the Glass Orchard, generally did what it was told.

Outside, beyond the conservatory walls, rain tapped at the remaining panes with the blunt patience of clerks.

Inside, the Orchard held its breath.

The air was thick enough to chew. It smelled of damp loam, dead lemons, and iron rust. Condensation gathered on the ribs of the greenhouse and dropped in slow, deliberate beads. Somewhere in the fern house, a pipe clicked. The sound moved through the structure, polite and hollow.

“Adrian,” Julianna said.

He did not turn.

Her voice came from the citrus walk, where the dead trees stood in two immaculate rows. Their branches were black, their leaves long fallen, their brass labels polished clean by his hand. Lisbon Lemon. Seville Orange. Bitter Blood. Varieties of a world that had once believed names could preserve things.

“She is not ours,” Julianna said.

The woman under the fig shifted. Her fingers scraped tile.

Adrian looked down at the key.

Its bow had been worn smooth by generations of Vey hands that had unlocked wine cellars, servants’ passages, winter gardens, drawing rooms, locked cabinets, and the nursery after Priscilla Roundhouse had decided locked doors made children imaginative. Green oxidation had bloomed near the shaft. The teeth were uneven. One had been bent since the year he dropped it into the fountain during his first summer alone.

The key opened nothing.

Every morning it opened everything.

The woman’s lips moved.

No sound came at first. Only a drag of breath, shallow and ragged. Adrian stepped closer before he had decided to. The sole of his shoe ground on glass powder.

Her eyes opened.

They were not right for the Orchard.

Too sharp. Too fevered. Too unwilling to arrange themselves into his expectations.

“Are you the owner?” she asked.

Her voice had a scraped quality, like a match struck on damp brick.

Adrian looked past her to the east transept door. It stood open behind the broken fig. The chain had been cut. Not rusted. Cut. A vulgar, practical interruption.

“I am the custodian,” he said.

She blinked. Rainwater ran from her lashes.

“Fine,” she said. “Custode me a blanket.”

Julianna made the small sound she made when guests failed to understand the tone of a place.

Adrian stepped back.

The woman tried to sit up. Her elbow slipped. Her teeth knocked together once, hard enough for him to hear. Not the delicate clatter of imagination. Not the pipe. Not the morning’s permitted sounds. Bone. Enamel. Cold.

“You have broken into private grounds,” he said.

“Yes.”

“There is a village three miles south.”

“Walked from it.”

“There is an inn there.”

“Closed.”

“There is a church.”

“Full of men who wanted my rings.”

He looked at her hands. No rings. Pale bands marked the fingers where they had been.

“You should return.”

She laughed once. It broke into a cough. The cough bent her forward until her forehead nearly touched the tile.

The Orchard received the sound badly. It moved up the glass ribs and came back thinner. Julianna’s dress did not stir.

Adrian put the key into his waistcoat pocket.

The woman saw the movement. Her eyes fixed on the pocket.

“Is that for doors,” she said, “or for theatre?”

Adrian should have left her there.

There were procedures for trespassers. Sidney had written them in her square hand and pinned them beside the kitchen entrance, though no one had come in seven years except the butcher’s boy and the man from the county with papers he had not been permitted to serve. Procedures were civilization. Civilization kept doors from becoming holes.

The woman’s skin had taken on a wax cast under the greenhouse light.

Adrian bent and reached for her arm.

Julianna said, “If you touch her, you will know.”

His hand stopped an inch above the woman’s sleeve.

The wool was black. Wet through. It smelled of rain, horse sweat, old perfume, and street smoke. She watched his hand hover. Something moved in her face that was not gratitude.

“Either do it,” she said, “or fetch someone less ornamental.”

Adrian took her arm.

She did not vanish.

Her sleeve was shockingly cold.

He pulled her upright. She made no complaint, which was worse than complaint would have been. Her weight came against him in a hard, shivering lean. A real body had no respect for composition. It sagged where it needed to sag. It forced balance to become a negotiation.

Julianna stood at the edge of the citrus walk, white gloved hands folded.

“You are making an opening,” she said.

Adrian dragged the woman toward the service stair.

“No,” he said.

The woman’s head rolled against his shoulder. “No what?”

“No conversation in the transept before eight.”

She looked at him sidelong, through soaked hair. “Naturally.”

He nearly dropped her on the first stair.

The service stair led beneath the Orchard, away from glass, away from the civic avenues, down through stone sweating mineral damp. The air changed with each step. Above, the greenhouse pressed its moist palm over everything. Below, the old boiler house breathed heat in uneven gusts.

The woman’s remaining shoe struck the iron edge of each stair: click, scrape, click, scrape.

That sound had brought her into the world. It had cut through the morning like a nail through silk. Adrian had been speaking to Julianna about the western fountain, which had developed a leak in the basin though it had held no water since Jackson Roundhouse’s funeral. The fountain committee had recommended temporary closure. Julianna had asked whether beauty should ever be closed for maintenance.

But then: click.

A shoe.

Not an imagined citizen’s measured tread. Not one of the glass children running in the palm house. Not Mayor Mike’s fussy cane.

A shoe with mud on it.

A shoe attached to an intruder.

Therefore the morning had split.

At the bottom of the stairs, Adrian pushed open the boiler-room door with his shoulder. Heat rolled out, thick and dry, smelling of coal dust, wet wool, and old velvet. The room was low and windowless. Pipes ran along the ceiling like blackened veins. The boiler sat at the far wall, round-bellied and red-eyed, making a deep animal thrum that pressed against the ribs.

Sidney kept it alive through winter by habit rather than necessity. The main house used modern radiators now, when anyone bothered to use the main house. The boiler remained for the greenhouse pipes, the old laundry, and Sidney’s distrust of improvements.

Adrian lowered the woman onto a wooden bench beside a stack of covered crates.

She folded around herself at once.

“Blanket,” she said.

He opened one of the crates. Mothballs stung the back of his nose. Inside lay theater drapes from Priscilla Roundhouse’s brief campaign to improve local culture by force. Scarlet velvet. Dusty gold braid. A painted canvas moon. Adrian pulled out the velvet and shook it once.

Dust rose. The woman sneezed.

“Sorry,” he said.

“Don’t waste it.”

He wrapped the velvet around her shoulders.

Her hands clutched it closed. Her fingers were thin and chapped. Not servant’s hands. Not field hands. Not hands that had expected to be used for weather.

“There is soup,” Adrian said.

“Is it imaginary?”

“No.”

“Then soup.”

He stood too quickly.

The key shifted in his pocket and struck his thigh.

He should have gone for Sidney. Therefore he went to the pantry himself, because bringing Sidney would make the woman official, and official things accumulated consequences. Sidney would ask questions. Sidney would look at him with her practical eyes and see what the Orchard had admitted.

The passage to the old kitchen was dark. One gas lamp hissed near the coal chute. Adrian passed shelves of preserves, drying herbs, covered jars, folded linen, hooks, pans, knives. Each object knew its purpose. None of them required ceremony.

He found broth in a lidded pot on the stove, thick with barley and onion. Sidney always left food where he could find it and never asked whether he had eaten the last. He filled a bowl, burned his thumb, cursed without voice, and took bread from the bin.

When he returned, the woman had removed her coat and one glove. The coat lay on the floor in a black heap. She had pushed the velvet close to the boiler, where steam pipes ticked overhead. Her hair, freed from its pins, hung in dark ropes against her face.

She was studying the room.

Not idly. Not as a guest.

As someone calculating exits.

He set the bowl beside her. She did not thank him. She ate with the spoon first, then with the bread, then raised the bowl and drank what remained. The smell of onion filled the space between them. Her throat moved. Heat put color into her mouth before it reached the rest of her.

Adrian stood with his hands behind his back.

“You may use the servants’ passage when the rain lightens,” he said.

She scraped the bowl with the bread.

“The chain on the east gate will be repaired.”

She chewed.

“There will be no complaint if nothing is taken.”

She looked up.

“Do you practice speeches,” she said, “or do they grow on you?”

The boiler gave a heavy knock. Steam moved through the pipes with a long, metallic sigh. For a moment, the room had no visible edges. Heat shimmered. The gas lamp fluttered. Adrian could not see the far wall clearly.

Blind, then, he heard her.

The spoon touching ceramic.

Her breath, steadier now.

Water dripping from her coat to the floor.

The boiler’s thrum.

No citizens. No fountain committee. No Julianna. The room permitted only what made sound.

“You have trespassed,” he said.

“I have.”

“You have cut a lock.”

“A chain.”

“You have entered a district not open to public visitation.”

She stared at him over the rim of the bowl.

Then she laughed. Not kindly. Not loudly. Enough to mark the absurdity and let it stand.

He took the bowl from her.

Her fingers brushed his.

He pulled back so quickly the spoon fell. It struck the floor and spun under the bench, silver flashing in the boiler light.

The woman watched him.

“There it is,” she said.

“What?”

“The rule.”

He held the bowl against his chest.

“There are no rules belowstairs.”

“You nearly broke your wrist avoiding my hand.”

He set the bowl on a crate.

“You are warm enough to leave.”

“No.”

The word landed flat.

Outside, thunder moved across the estate, distant but approaching. The boiler answered with its own dull complaint. The woman pulled the velvet higher and leaned back against the wall. Her face had sharpened as heat returned to it. Cheekbones. Hollow temples. A small scar at the corner of her lip. A person assembled from facts he had not authorized.

“You know me,” he said.

She looked at the coal scuttle.

The silence had texture. It had ash in it.

“No,” she said.

The lie was badly made.

Adrian put his hand into his pocket and closed it around the key. Its coldness had survived the boiler room. A private piece of winter.

“No one comes here by accident.”

“I did.”

“The village road is marked.”

“I left the road.”

“The estate wall is posted.”

“I did not pause to admire the typography.”

“You cut the chain.”

“Yes.”

“With what?”

She looked toward her coat.

He crossed the room and lifted it. Something heavy slid from the pocket and struck the floor. A small pair of bolt cutters. New. Cheap. Effective.

He picked them up.

“Prepared accident,” he said.

She leaned her head against the wall and closed her eyes.

A drop of water fell from a pipe into a tin basin. Plink. Plink. Plink.

“You are Adrian Roundhouse,” she said.

The name did not belong in the boiler room.

It had belonged in newspapers. Court transcripts. Society columns first, then criminal proceedings, then editorials with moral verbs. It had belonged to men at club windows lowering their voices. It had belonged to ladies who knew exactly when not to know someone. It had belonged to a judge who pronounced it as if checking the fit of a coffin lid.

Here, below the Orchard, spoken by a wet woman in stolen heat, it sounded unfinished.

Adrian turned the bolt cutters in his hands.

“You denied knowing me.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“You looked as if the name might break something.”

“Names do not break things.”

“No,” she said. “People do. Names are just where we hang the pieces.”

The boiler hissed.

He should have sent for Sidney. Therefore he did not move. The woman had brought the outside into the house, but she had also brought information. Information had weight. It could not be dragged back up the stair and left beneath the fig.

“How do you know me?” he asked.

Her eyes opened.

“I was at the north gallery hearing.”

He remembered the gallery.

Not faces. Hats. Feathers. A row of pale ovals above black collars. The smell of varnished wood. Rain on courthouse windows. His own gloves folded in his lap because his solicitor had advised stillness. The prosecution model of the collapsed building stood on a table like a dollhouse after a tantrum.

“You testified?”

“No.”

“Then you came to watch.”

“I came because Alonzo Altemere owned shares in the development company.”

A pipe knocked overhead. Once. Twice.

The development company. There it was. No need to name the building. The dead had been named enough by others. Twelve names. Printed in columns. Spoken in committees. Etched on a plaque he had never seen because the city had not invited him to the unveiling.

“Alonzo Altemere,” he said.

“Ruined himself later. Not from conscience. From arithmetic.”

Adrian looked at her hands again. Ring bands. Thin wrists. A socialite’s coat gone black at the seams.

“What is your name?”

She pulled the velvet tighter.

“Nora Altemere.”

The Vale family had occupied the second row during sentencing. Jeannie Altemere had worn a veil though she had lost no one. Her father had stared straight ahead, jaw set with the offended dignity of men who survived disasters by donating blame.

“Nora Altemere,” he said.

“You remember.”

“No.”

“You do.”

He walked to the coal chute and back. The bolt cutters were still in his hand. He set them on a crate.

“You believed me guilty.”

“No.”

The word came too quickly.

He laughed once. It surprised him. The sound had no practice.

“No,” he repeated.

“I didn’t.”

“But you said nothing.”

Her face changed by degrees. The boiler painted one side of it red. The other remained in gaslight.

“I was nineteen.”

“That is a number. Not an answer.”

“Alonzo Altemere said the inquiry was complicated.”

“Fathers often do.”

“He said your design had irregularities.”

“My design was altered after approval.”

“I know that now.”

“Now is a generous country. Everyone applies for citizenship eventually.”

She flinched at that. Not much. Enough.

Adrian felt the key’s shape through his pocket though his hand was no longer on it. The pocket seemed to hold a small, hard pulse.

Nora looked at the spoon under the bench. She reached for it, failed, and withdrew her hand.

“I came because I heard you kept staff,” she said.

“I do.”

“One housekeeper. An old one. People in the village say she feeds strays.”

“Sidney feeds useful creatures.”

“Then I overestimated myself.”

Her mouth tightened around the sentence. Not self-pity. Something drier. A fact presented badly because it had teeth.

He should have disliked her more for it.

Above them, the Orchard creaked.

The storm had found the glass.

A long scrape moved across the greenhouse roof, branch on pane. Rain hardened. What had been tapping became drumming. The boiler room dimmed as the gas flame guttered under a draft from somewhere it should not have had a draft.

Adrian looked at the ceiling.

The citrus walk.

The east transept.

The west arcade.

Districts opened, citizens abroad, morning ritual incomplete.

Julianna waiting.

“You cannot remain here,” he said.

Nora closed her eyes again.

“Then drag me back into the rain.”

“It is not a question of rain.”

“No,” she said. “It never is with men like you.”

He looked at her.

“Men like me.”

“Men with private kingdoms. Men who make rules no one else has agreed to obey.”

“This is my house.”

“This is a greenhouse full of dead trees.”

“The Orchard is not dead.”

“Then it is doing an excellent imitation.”

The sentence struck something load-bearing.

Adrian crossed to the door.

Nora said, “Where are you going?”

“To close the upper vents.”

“That is not what you’re doing.”

He opened the door.

The stairwell breathed wet air down at him. The humidity had returned, but it was colder now, threaded with rain. From above came the soft clatter of loose panes in their frames. The Orchard speaking in teeth.

He climbed.

Behind him, the bench scraped. Nora stood or tried to.

“Adrian.”

The name again.

He gripped the rail.

She said, “There was a memorandum.”

He stopped.

Rain hammer the glass overhead.

“What memorandum?”

“Alonzo Altemere had it copied. After the collapse. Internal correspondence from the contractor. They knew about the substitutions. The cheaper ribs. The load variance.”

The stairwell narrowed.

He could smell wet iron, coal smoke, and the velvet’s mildew rising behind him.

“You saw this memorandum?”

“Yes.”

“During the trial?”

“After the first week.”

The key in his pocket became heavier than the house.

“You said nothing.”

“Alonzo Altemere said it was inadmissible. Stolen. Partial. He said it would only confuse the record.”

“The record,” Adrian said.

His voice did not echo. The stairwell swallowed it.

“I was nineteen,” she said again.

“Stop offering me your age.”

She stood three steps below him, one hand on the wall, wrapped in red velvet like a failed actress in a cellar production. Her hair had begun to dry in uneven waves. The practical sharpness had gone from her face. What remained was worse because it asked for no arrangement.

“He burned it,” she said.

Adrian resumed climbing.

“Nora did not exist in the Orchard,” Julianna would say. “There is no memorandum. There are no fathers below the citrus walk. There is no trial before noon.”

But the stairwell smelled of wet velvet.

The woman had said memorandum.

Therefore the Orchard shifted.

At the top, the service door banged open in a gust. Cold rain blew into his face. The east transept had changed while he was below. One of the upper panes had shattered, and rain fell in a slant through the gap, striking the fig leaves with bright, violent ticks. The dead tree trembled under weather. Glass glittered on the tiles.

Julianna stood beyond the rain in the citrus walk.

Her white dress remained dry.

“Adrian,” she said.

He stepped into the transept.

The brass key struck his thigh with each step.

Julianna’s face was calm, or rather he had made it calm after the first year because he could not bear another face learning how to leave. She had the brow of the woman who had returned his letters unopened, the mouth of the one who had lowered her eyes in court, the hands of a girl he had walked beside before buildings learned to fall down. She was assembled from the usable portions of several betrayals. A more efficient woman than the world had provided.

“You should not have taken her below,” Julianna said.

“She was cold.”

“Cold passes.”

“Sometimes.”

“She knows your name.”

“So do you.”

“I was given it kindly.”

A pane cracked overhead.

Adrian looked up.

The crack ran from corner to corner like lightning trapped in glass. Rain bulged against it. Wind pressed. The pane held for one second, two, three.

Then broke.

Glass came down in a silver sheet.

Adrian jumped back. Shards struck tile where he had stood. Rain rushed through the opening. The Orchard exhaled, or the world inhaled, and the difference could no longer be maintained.

From the west arcade came voices.

Mayor Mike, indignant about emergency procedures.

Mrs. Wead, demanding sacks for flour that did not exist.

The glass children laughing too close to broken edges.

Julianna lifted one hand.

“They are frightened,” she said.

No one in the Orchard had ever been frightened unless he assigned them a tolerable civic concern. A missing dog. A school roof. A cracked fountain basin. Not this. Not weather entering without permit.

Adrian ran toward the west arcade.

Behind him, from the service door, Nora shouted his name.

The arcade was slick underfoot. Rain slide down the inside of the glass walls. Leaves long dead broke loose from their branches and skated across the tiles. Iron ribs groaned overhead. Another pane shattered, then another. The storm had found the weaknesses and was reading them aloud.

Citizens crowded the arcade.

Not fully. Never fully. Coats, faces, hats, hands, impressions made urgent. Mayor Mike held his cane in both hands. Mrs. Wead’s apron was white with impossible flour. The glass children ran barefoot through shards without bleeding.

“Order,” Adrian said.

His voice vanished into the storm.

The brass key was in his hand now though he did not remember drawing it. He held it up.

“Order.”

Mayor Mike looked through him.

That had never happened.

Mrs. Wead turned toward the bakery district that had no ovens. The children fled toward the palm house. The whole city of Mercy was moving without him.

But Julianna remained beneath the central dome.

Water poured behind her in bright cords. Her white dress stirred at the hem. Her hair, pinned as always, did not loosen. She raised her hand toward him.

“Adrian,” she said. “Do not let me go.”

He knew the rule.

He had made it after the first winter, when loneliness had become too crowded and the citizens had begun appearing in doorways uninvited. A rule was needed. A boundary. Conversation was permitted. Proximity was permitted. Touch was forbidden.

If touched, they vanished.

It was not cruelty. It was architecture.

A world needed load-bearing absences.

He stepped over broken glass.

The key’s teeth cut into his palm.

“Nora,” someone shouted behind him.

No. Not someone.

Nora.

She had followed him out of the boiler house. The red velvet dragged behind her through puddles. Her bare foot left pale prints among the shards. She had wrapped one hand in her discarded coat and held it like a shield over her head.

“Get away from there,” she shouted.

Julianna’s hand remained extended.

The dome above her gave a low, metallic pop.

Adrian looked up.

One of the iron braces had twisted. The old central panes shuddered under wind pressure. Rain streamed through the seams. The dome had been designed to distribute weight in four directions. He knew the lines. He knew the stress. He saw, with the old professional eye that had survived disgrace because ruin does not unteach geometry, where the failure would begin.

Above Julianna.

Therefore he ran to her.

Nora shouted again, but her voice came from behind rain and iron.

Julianna smiled.

Not the smile of women in courtrooms.

Not the smile of ladies who wrote no replies.

Not the smile of fathers explaining admissibility.

The smile he had designed after all smiles had become suspicious.

He reached for her hand.

“Nora,” he said, though he did not mean to.

Something struck him from the side.

Not glass.

A body.

Nora slammed into him with both arms around his waist. They hit the tile together and slid. The key flew from his hand, skittered across the floor, struck the base of a dead lemon tree, and spun once before lying still.

Above them, the central pane gave way.

Glass crashed where Adrian had been standing.

The sound emptied the world.

For several seconds there was only impact. Rain. Iron. Nora’s breath against his shoulder. The boiler heat gone from her, replaced by storm cold and wet wool. Her fingers locked in his coat. Her weight pinning him.

He did not move.

She did not vanish.

The key lay ten feet away.

Julianna stood beyond the fallen glass.

Then she did not.

There was no burst, no cry, no theatrical departure. The rain occupied the place where she had been. The dead citrus trees stood behind it. Water ran along the tiles, carrying black leaves and glass splinters toward the drain.

Adrian stared.

Nora lifted her head.

A cut ran along her cheek, thin and bright. Rain diluted it at once.

“You idiot,” she said.

The words came out shaking.

He looked at her hands gripping his coat.

Her fingers were real. Red at the knuckles. One nail torn. A thread from his waistcoat caught under her thumb.

“You touched me,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I touched you.”

“No. I tackled you. Accuracy matters.”

“You did not vanish.”

She looked at him as if considering whether the fallen glass had done some delayed work inside his skull.

“No,” she said.

He turned his head toward the lemon tree.

The brass key lay in a puddle. Rain struck it, bounced off, and gathered in its worn bow.

Mayor Mike was gone.

Mrs. Wead was gone.

The glass children were gone.

Or perhaps the rain was too loud to hear them. Perhaps morning visitation had ended. Perhaps emergency procedures had moved all citizens to the lower district. Perhaps Julianna had stepped behind the fountain. Perhaps the city of Mercy remained exactly where it had been, awaiting reopening, requiring only the key, the ritual, the phrase at the east gate.

Nora released his coat and pushed herself upright.

The velvet had come loose from her shoulders. It lay soaked around them, red darkened almost to black.

She followed his gaze to the key.

“Leave it,” she said.

He did not answer.

The dome groaned.

Nora slapped his shoulder. Not hard. Enough.

“Below,” she said.

The word had no metaphor in it.

They crawled first, then stood. Adrian’s legs moved badly. The Orchard had become louder than thought. Rain hit tile. Wind tore at broken panes. Iron complained. Somewhere, water poured steadily into a basin that had been dry for years.

He reached for the key as they passed the lemon tree.

Nora caught his wrist.

Her hand closed around bare skin.

The contact stopped him more completely than the falling dome had.

Her grip was cold. Her pulse beat against his wrist, fast and uneven. Not vanishing. Not symbolic. A small engine insisting on its own fact.

“Leave it,” she said again.

He looked at the key.

Then at her hand.

Then he left it.

They reached the service stair as another section of glass came down behind them.

In the stairwell, the storm became muffled. The boiler’s thrum rose from below, steady and vulgar and alive. Nora descended ahead of him, one hand on the wall, the other pressed to her cut cheek. Adrian followed with nothing in his hands.

The boiler room received them with heat.

Not kindly. Functionally.

Nora dropped onto the bench. Adrian shut the door and leaned against it. Water ran from his hair into his collar. His palm throbbed where the key had cut it. He opened his hand.

Four small dents. One line of blood.

Nora saw.

“Come here,” she said.

He did not.

She leaned sideways, opened a crate, and pulled free a strip of gold-braided cloth from one of Priscilla Roundhouse’s old costumes. She held it out.

“You are bleeding.”

“It is small.”

“Most openings start that way.”

He crossed the room.

She wrapped the cloth around his palm. Her fingers were clumsy with cold, but the knot held. The braid glittered absurdly against his skin. Ceremonial bandage. Theatre pressed into service.

For a while neither spoke.

The boiler filled the room. Its heat dried nothing quickly. Wet wool steamed. Velvet soured. Coal dust stuck to the water on the floor. Above them, the Orchard broke in stages, but down here the sound arrived as dull reports, remote and bureaucratic.

Nora sat back.

“I saw the memorandum,” she said.

Adrian looked at the bandage.

“Not now.”

“Yes. Now.”

He lifted his eyes.

Her face had gone pale again, but not with cold this time. The cut on her cheek had stopped bleeding. A strand of hair clung to it.

“Alonzo Altemere burned it,” she said. “I watched him do it in the library grate. He told me men like you always survive. He said families like ours did not. He said the dead were already dead. He said the trial needed a shape people could understand.”

Adrian said nothing.

The boiler knocked.

“I believed you,” she said. “Not enough to speak. Enough to know I was keeping silent.”

He sat on the crate opposite her.

The room smelled of coal, wet velvet, onions from the empty bowl, and blood in a quantity too small to matter except to the hand that owned it.

“Why come here?” he asked.

She looked at the floor.

“Because Alonzo Altemere was wrong. You did not survive.”

The answer remained between them.

No apology followed. Perhaps she had none ready. Perhaps apology was another room in a house that had burned. Perhaps words like that required a floor more level than this one.

Adrian flexed his bandaged hand.

“There was a woman,” he said.

Nora looked up.

“In the papers?”

“No.”

“In the Orchard?”

He did not answer.

“What was her name?”

He looked toward the ceiling.

The city of Mercy had gone silent. The silence did not feel empty. It felt wet, damaged, waiting to see what he would claim.

“Julianna,” he said.

“Was she real?”

“Parts of her.”

Nora nodded as if this were a category she understood.

“She told me not to help you,” he said.

“Sound woman.”

“She said you were not ours.”

“I’m not.”

“No.”

Nora looked at his bandaged hand. Then at his face.

“What happens now?” she asked.

He almost reached for his pocket.

The pocket was empty.

The movement ended before it began.

Above them, something gave way with a crash that seemed final until the next crash corrected it. Rain poured somewhere in great volume. The house would flood if the drains clogged. The fig would split under the weight. The citrus walk would be ruined beyond preservation. Insurance men would have to be called. Men with boots. Men with notebooks. Men who touched gates without permission.

“Now,” Adrian said, "the upper valves must be shut.”

She stared at him.

“That’s your answer?”

“Yes.”

“Your glass cathedral is collapsing, I confess to helping bury evidence that destroyed your life, and your answer is plumbing?”

“The valves feed the roof tanks. If they continue, the west arcade will flood.”

She looked at him for a long moment.

Then she laughed.

Not cruelly this time. Not kindly either. More like the body discovering one usable muscle after a long illness.

“Fine,” she said. “Custode the valves.”

“You cannot walk barefoot upstairs.”

“I can if the alternative is drowning in symbolism.”

He stood.

“There are boots in the laundry.”

“Good.”

“And a coat.”

“Better.”

“And Sidney.”

Nora’s expression shifted.

“She will ask questions,” Adrian said.

“Women do.”

“She will feed you.”

Nora looked away.

The boiler thrum filled the space where pride might have staged an argument.

“Then fetch Sidney,” she said.

Adrian opened the boiler-room door.

Warm air moved past him into the stairwell. Cold air came down in exchange. The house had begun bargaining with the weather. Fairly, perhaps. Late, certainly.

He climbed alone.

At the top, he paused.

The Orchard was no longer a town.

It was a greenhouse after a storm, though the storm had not finished. Broken panes. Dead trees. Water on tile. Iron ribs exposed. The east transept door open to rain. The central dome breached. The citrus walk littered with glass.

The brass key still lay beneath the lemon tree.

He went to it.

For a moment he stood over the key and listened.

Rain.

Glass ticking as it cooled and shifted.

Wind in dead branches.

A distant pipe complaining.

No mayor. No children. No Julianna.

He bent, picked up the key, and felt its familiar weight settle into his hand. Cold brass. Worn bow. Bent tooth. Green oxidation. Nothing more. Nothing less.

He could put it in his pocket.

He could return tomorrow at seven.

He could open the east gate, apologize for the interruption, convene an emergency session, rebuild Mercy with stricter boundaries. No trespassers. No Vales. No memoranda. Julianna restored beneath the dome, perhaps with a new dress appropriate to storm damage.

The key waited.

Objects were patient with cowards.

Adrian closed his fist around it and went down through the service passage, not to the boiler room, but to the rear yard.

Rain struck his face hard. The yard behind the greenhouse was mud, gravel, nettles, collapsed planters, broken slates. The old ash pit stood beside the boiler vent, half full of cinders and rainwater.

He knelt.

The mud accepted his knees without ceremony.

With his bandaged hand, he dug a shallow hole beside the ash pit. The gold braid darkened, came loose, and stuck to the wound. He placed the key in the mud.

Then he stopped.

Burial was too clean.

Too theatrical.

A final act for an audience he no longer had.

He took the key back.

Mud filled the bow. Rain washed some of it away.

When he returned to the boiler room, Nora had found the spoon and was holding it against her cut cheek because the metal was cool. She looked at his muddy knees, then at the key in his hand.

“Couldn’t do it?” she asked.

“No.”

He sat opposite her and placed the key on the floor between them.

Not near himself.

Not near her.

Between.

Sidney’s steps sounded in the passage above. Heavy. Quick. Accompanied by the unmistakable rhythm of a woman already preparing to call two people fools in the order of their injury.

Nora looked at the key.

“What does it open?” she asked.

Adrian listened to the storm moving through the broken Orchard, to Sidney descending, to the boiler keeping its red, practical eye on the room.

“Less than I thought,” he said.
